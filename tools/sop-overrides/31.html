Core principles

  • Stop, don't rush. A fast lift off the floor causes more harm than a few minutes of assessment.
  • Never lift a client off the floor. Not by hand, not with a co-worker. Atria has no floor lifts. If the client cannot get up themselves with coaching, call 911.
  • Don't catch a falling client. Guide them down along your body and protect their head. Catching an adult injures you both.
  • Every fall is reported, including falls with no visible injury, and falls the client asks you not to report.

Prevention, every visit

  1. Check the path. Clear walkways, cords and clutter; wipe spills.
  2. Check the person. Footwear, glasses, and the mobility aid's brakes, tips and height.
  3. Check the room. Lighting, especially the route to the toilet at night, and the call device within reach.
  4. Check the plan. The RN completes a falls risk assessment at intake and after every fall, and writes the fall plan into the care plan.

Part A: a fall in the home

  1. Stop and make it safe. Don't rush in. Look for water, broken glass, a stove left on, pets, or a walker tangled underneath. An injured worker cannot help anyone.
  2. Check responsiveness and breathing. Say their name and tap their shoulder. Not responding, or not breathing normally: call 911, put the phone on speaker, and follow the dispatcher. Start CPR if trained. If there is a DNR Confirmation Form on file, tell 911 and follow it.
  3. Keep them still and ask. Get down to their level. What happened — did they trip, or feel dizzy or black out first? Where does it hurt? Did they hit their head? How long have they been on the floor? Then look them over head to toe without moving them.
  4. Check for red flags. Any one of the signs below means they stay where they are: call 911, then your supervisor. If you are not sure whether something counts, treat it as one.

  1. Protect them while you wait. Keep them still and warm, with blankets over them and something between them and a cold floor. Never roll them to slide something underneath. No pillow if the head, neck or back is involved. Press gently on bleeding; cover skin tears without pulling the flap off. Nothing to eat, drink or take by mouth — they may need surgery. Help with continence needs. Have the medication list ready; paramedics ask about blood thinners first. Never leave them alone on the floor.
  2. Decide on getting up — all three must be true. No red flags and no new pain moving arms and legs. The client wants to get up and feels able. They can do it themselves with your coaching, or with a client-owned floor-recovery device you are trained on. Any "no": protect them and call 911.
  3. Coach with words, not your back. Roll onto their side, onto hands and knees, crawl to a sturdy chair, one foot flat, push up to sit. You steady the chair; you do not lift. Rest at least 10 minutes, then recheck pain, dizziness, confusion and breathing. Anything new: call 911.
  4. Report before you leave. Call the supervisor for every fall, even with no injury, and even if the client objects.
  5. Document the same shift. Incident report, facts only: "found on the floor beside the bed", not "fell out of bed", unless someone saw it.

Part B: a fall outside the home

On a walk, at an appointment, in a shop or a car park, Part A still applies in full — steps 1 to 9, in the same order. What changes is that you control none of the conditions and carry none of the equipment, so you call for help sooner.

  1. Danger first. Check for cars, ice, stairs and crowds before you go to them. Move a client only if staying put is dangerous, such as in moving traffic, and only as far as needed.
  2. Call 911 earlier than you would indoors. Hard, cold or wet ground and no chair or bed to work with mean a long lie becomes dangerous much faster outside. If in doubt, call.
  3. Getting up is usually off the table. The floor-recovery method needs a sturdy chair at the right height that will not slide, and dry, level footing. A trolley rolls and a shop floor is often wet. Outside, even with no red flags, the honest answer is usually to wait for paramedics.
  4. Give the exact location. Address or nearest intersection, shop name, which entrance, which parking level. Ask someone to meet the ambulance.
  5. Keep them warm and dry. Coats or blankets over and around them; ask shop staff for blankets. In summer heat, get them into shade.
  6. Stop well-meaning helpers. Strangers reach straight for the armpits. Put yourself between them and the client, thank them, and give them another job: "Thank you, please don't lift her. Paramedics are coming. Could you watch for the ambulance?"
  7. Never leave them to get help. Call from where you are. Ask nearby staff for a first aid kit or an AED. If they insist they are fine, you cannot force care: explain your concern, stay with them, get them home safely if there are no red flags, and report it like any other fall.

After any fall: watch for 24 to 72 hours

At every visit after a fall, report straight away: new or worse pain, headache, vomiting, drowsiness or new confusion, new trouble walking or bearing weight, new bruising or swelling. Brain bleeds and fractures can appear hours or days later.

Documentation: incident report the same shift, post-fall monitoring notes at each visit for 72 hours, and an updated falls risk assessment and fall plan by the RN.
